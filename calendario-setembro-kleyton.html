The 183 days that make you a US taxpayer

Páginas do PDF — texto na peça + direção de arte
01Capa
"You don't need a Green Card to become a US taxpayer. You need days."
Fundo escuro, tipografia condensada muito grande. Sem logo nesta página. A capa é uma afirmação, não uma peça de marca.
02O que está
em jogo
What US tax residency actually changes
Texto: "It is not about immigration status. A US tax resident reports worldwide income to the IRS and discloses foreign financial accounts — including companies, investments and assets held outside the country." Arte: globo simples com um recorte destacando os EUA.
03A regra
The substantial presence test
Texto: "Two conditions have to be met in the same year: at least 31 days of physical presence in the current year, and 183 days across a three-year window." Arte: duas condições lado a lado com um sinal de "+" entre elas.
04A pegadinha
The three years aren't weighted equally
Texto: "Days in the current year count in full. Days in the previous year count as one third. Days in the year before that count as one sixth. This is where the math surprises people." Arte: três barras de alturas diferentes, rotuladas 1 · 1/3 · 1/6.
05O exemplo
120 days a year is fine. 130 is not.
Texto: "120 days for three consecutive years: 120 + 40 + 20 = 180. Below the threshold. 130 days for three consecutive years: 130 + 43 + 22 = 195. Above it. Ten days a year is the entire difference." Arte: duas colunas de cálculo, a segunda destacada em âmbar.
06As saídas
The exceptions are not automatic
Texto: "There are relief routes for people who meet the test but have a closer connection to another country, and for residents of countries with a US tax treaty. Both have to be claimed on a filing. Neither happens by itself." Arte: duas portas, ambas com um cadeado pequeno e a palavra "filed" ao lado.
07Pergunta
"How many days have you spent in the US over the last three years — counted, not estimated?"
Fundo claro, pergunta grande, logo KwikLedgers discreto no rodapé. Sem botão e sem URL.
Legenda
You can become a US taxpayer without ever applying for anything. Not a Green Card. Not a visa change. Just days. US tax residency is decided by a physical presence test, and once you meet it, the consequences are wide: worldwide income reported to the IRS, and foreign financial accounts, companies and investments disclosed. The test has two parts that must be met in the same year. At least 31 days of presence in the current year, and 183 days across a three-year window. The second part is where people get caught, because the three years don't count equally. Current year: every day counts in full. Previous year: each day counts as one third. The year before that: each day counts as one sixth. Run the math on a routine that looks harmless. 120 days a year, three years running: 120 + 40 + 20 = 180. You're under. 130 days a year, three years running: 130 + 43 + 22 = 195. You're over. Ten days a year is the whole difference between a business trip pattern and a worldwide reporting obligation. There are relief routes — for people with a closer connection to another country, and for residents of treaty countries. Both have to be claimed on a filing. Neither applies automatically, and neither applies retroactively to a year nobody looked at. If you run a business outside the US and travel here regularly, this is worth counting rather than assuming. How many days have you spent in the US over the last three years — counted, or estimated?

Two obligations, one date

September 15 is two deadlines wearing the same date. The first is the Q3 estimated tax payment. Miss it and the penalty accrues quietly, in the background, showing up months later attached to a number you didn't expect. The second is the extended filing deadline for calendar-year partnerships and S corporations that took an extension back in March. They come from opposite directions, which is why most owners have exactly one of them on the calendar. If you're a partner in an entity and you also pay your own estimated tax, both land on you the same day. Twelve days out is still early. Next week it's a scramble. Which of the two is already handled on your side?

"No profit. No tax owed. A five-figure penalty anyway."
Fundo escuro, três frases empilhadas, a última em âmbar. Sem logo.
02Quem
é atingido
Who this applies to
Texto: "A US LLC with a single owner who is not a US person. The structure thousands of foreign entrepreneurs open every year — often the simplest one their advisor recommended." Arte: silhueta de uma empresa com uma seta vindo de fora do país.
03O paradoxo
Disregarded for tax. Not disregarded for reporting.
Texto: "For income tax purposes this entity is treated as if it didn't exist separately from its owner. That is exactly why owners assume there is nothing to file. The reporting obligation runs on a different track." Arte: duas trilhas paralelas, uma esmaecida e outra em destaque.
04A obrigação
An information return, filed on its own
Texto: "The entity has to report its transactions with its foreign owner and with related parties, on a dedicated form attached to a return the entity files purely for this purpose." Arte: um formulário grampeado a outro, ícone simples.
05O que conta
como transação
The list is broader than people expect
Texto: "It is not limited to sales. Money the owner puts into the company and money the owner takes out are reportable. So are loans, and amounts paid on the entity's behalf. Many owners with 'no activity' still have reportable transactions." Arte: quatro ícones em linha — aporte, retirada, empréstimo, pagamento por conta.
06A penalidade
A fixed amount, unrelated to what you earned
Texto: "The penalty for failing to file is a flat five-figure amount per year, and it does not scale down because the company was dormant or unprofitable. It is one of the few penalties in the US system with no relationship to income." Arte: número grande, com "per year" pequeno ao lado.
07O erro
que se repete
"We didn't operate, so we didn't file"
Texto: "The most common version of this problem isn't evasion. It's an owner who opened the entity, funded it, didn't trade, and reasonably concluded there was nothing to report." Arte: fundo âmbar claro, frase entre aspas grandes.
08Pergunta
"If you own a US LLC from abroad — do you know whether this filing was made for last year?"
Fundo claro, pergunta grande, logo KwikLedgers discreto no rodapé.
Legenda
A US LLC with a foreign owner can have no revenue, no profit and no tax due — and still owe a five-figure penalty for a form nobody mentioned. Here's the setup that produces it. A single-member LLC owned by someone who isn't a US person is disregarded for income tax purposes. Treated as if it doesn't exist separately from its owner. Which is precisely why owners conclude there's nothing to file. The income tax treatment and the reporting obligation run on different tracks. The entity has to report its transactions with its foreign owner and related parties, on a dedicated information form, attached to a return the entity files for that purpose alone. And "transactions" is broader than it sounds. It isn't limited to sales. Money the owner puts in is reportable. Money the owner takes out is reportable. So are loans and amounts paid on the entity's behalf. Which means the classic case — "we opened it, funded it, never operated" — usually has reportable transactions anyway. The penalty for missing it is a flat annual amount in the five figures. It doesn't scale down for a dormant company. It has no relationship to income, because it isn't an income tax penalty at all. This is rarely evasion. It's almost always someone who opened a structure abroad, was told it was the simple option, and was never told what the simple option still requires. If you own a US LLC from outside the country: do you know whether this filing was made for last year?

Taxed on money that never reached your account

01Capa
"The business had its best year. You left the money in. You still owe the tax."
Fundo escuro, três frases empilhadas em ritmo curto. Sem logo.
02A mecânica
How pass-through taxation actually works
Texto: "Partnerships and S corporations don't pay federal income tax. They report each owner's share of income on a Schedule K-1, and the owner carries those figures onto a personal return — whether or not any money moved." Arte: diagrama entidade → K-1 → declaração pessoal.
03A distinção
Allocated is not distributed
Texto: "Your K-1 reports your share of what the entity earned. Not your share of what it paid you. Reinvest the profit into inventory, equipment or hiring and the earnings are still allocated to you." Arte: duas colunas contrastantes, "allocated" cheia e "distributed" quase vazia.
04Onde
vira problema
Growth consumes its own profit
Texto: "The better the year, the larger the allocation — and the more likely the owner has a tax bill with no corresponding deposit to pay it from. Strong years produce this more often than weak ones." Arte: duas linhas num gráfico, lucro subindo e caixa estável.
05O espelho
Losses aren't automatically deductible either
Texto: "An owner can generally only deduct losses up to their basis in the entity. Beyond that limit the loss suspends and waits — sometimes for years, until basis is restored." Arte: barra com um limite marcado, excedente esmaecido.
06O que fazer
antes de dez
Three things that prevent the April version of this conversation
Texto: "Know the projected allocation before year-end, not when the K-1 arrives. Agree with your partners, in writing, how tax distributions work. Track basis continuously instead of reconstructing it under deadline." Arte: três linhas com marcadores simples.
07Pergunta
"Do you have a rough idea of what will be allocated to you this year — or will the K-1 be the first you hear of it?"
Fundo claro, pergunta grande, logo KwikLedgers discreto no rodapé.
Legenda
The business had its best year yet. You left the money in to fund growth. In April you owe tax on income that never reached your personal account. That's not a mistake. That's how pass-through taxation works, and it catches owners every year. Partnerships and S corporations don't pay federal income tax. They allocate income to the owners on a Schedule K-1, and the owner reports it personally — regardless of whether any cash moved. Allocated is not distributed. Your K-1 reports your share of what the entity earned, not your share of what it paid out. Which sets up a specific trap for businesses that are growing well. Growth consumes profit — inventory, equipment, headcount. The stronger the year, the larger the allocation, and the more likely the tax bill arrives with no deposit behind it. The same principle runs the other direction, and fewer people know this one: losses aren't automatically deductible either. You can generally only deduct a loss up to your basis in the entity. Past that, it suspends and waits. Three things prevent the April version of this conversation: Know the projected allocation before December, not when the K-1 lands. Agree with your partners, in writing, how tax distributions work. Track basis as you go instead of reconstructing it under deadline. The K-1s issued on September 15 are already moving toward individual returns due October 15. The allocation on them was decided months ago. Do you have a rough idea of what will be allocated to you this year — or will the K-1 be the first you hear of it?

The entity question a foreign founder should actually be asking

Páginas do PDF — texto na peça + direção de arte
01Capa
"Every entity comparison you've read was written for someone who lives here."
Fundo escuro, tipografia grande. Sem logo.
02O problema
da comparação
The usual comparison answers the wrong question
Texto: "Flexibility, paperwork, investor readiness. All real considerations — and all written on the assumption that the owner is a US person. For a founder who isn't, a different variable outranks every one of them." Arte: uma tabela comparativa clássica com um X grande por cima.
03A variável
que manda
Where you are taxed, not just how much
Texto: "The structure you choose determines whether US income lands on you personally in the US system, how much is withheld before money leaves the country, and how the result interacts with your obligations at home." Arte: três engrenagens conectadas, rotuladas residência · retenção · país de origem.
04Consequência 1
Personal exposure to the US system
Texto: "Some structures push US income directly onto the owner's personal position in the US. Others keep it at the entity level. That single difference changes what you file, where, and under whose name." Arte: duas setas, uma indo para uma silhueta de pessoa, outra parando numa caixa.
05Consequência 2
What happens when money leaves the country
Texto: "Payments from a US entity to a foreign owner can carry withholding before they arrive. Whether they do, and at what rate, depends on the structure, the type of payment, and whether a tax treaty applies." Arte: uma linha cruzando uma fronteira com um pedaço destacado em âmbar.
06Consequência 3
The other country has an opinion too
Texto: "A structure that is efficient on the US side can create a worse result at home. The only meaningful evaluation is the combined one — and it is almost never the one people run." Arte: duas bandeiras estilizadas, sem identificação de país, com um sinal de igual entre elas.
07A ordem
certa
Ask these before asking which entity
Texto: "Where will you be a tax resident over the next three years? Will profit be distributed or reinvested? Will you have US employees or US customers, or neither? Do you need outside investment? The entity answer falls out of these." Arte: quatro perguntas numeradas em coluna.
08Pergunta
"Was your structure chosen with both countries on the table — or only one?"
Fundo claro, pergunta grande, logo KwikLedgers discreto no rodapé.
Legenda
Every entity comparison you've read was written for someone who lives here. Flexibility. Paperwork. Investor readiness. Pass-through versus corporate treatment. All real considerations — and all built on the assumption that the owner is a US person. If you're not, a different variable outranks every one of them: where you end up being taxed, and under whose name. Three consequences the standard comparison doesn't cover. The first is personal exposure. Some structures push US income directly onto the owner's personal position inside the US system. Others keep it at the entity level. That difference decides what you file, where, and in whose name — before anyone talks about rates. The second is what happens when money leaves the country. Payments from a US entity to a foreign owner can carry withholding before they arrive. Whether they do, and how much, depends on the structure, the type of payment, and whether a treaty applies. The third is that the other country has an opinion. A structure that looks efficient on the US side can produce a worse combined result at home. The only evaluation that means anything is the combined one, and it's almost never the one that gets run. Which is why the entity question is the wrong place to start. These come first: Where will you be a tax resident over the next three years? Will profit be distributed, or reinvested? Will you have US employees, US customers, both, or neither? Do you need outside investment? Answer those four and the entity answer usually falls out on its own. Was your structure chosen with both countries on the table — or only one?

Your registered agent is not an office

24/set (qui) · publicar 9h–10h ET (10h–11h BRT) · imagem estática 1080×1350

Arte — o que vai na imagem
Fundo
Fundo escuro sólido. Composição em duas metades separadas por um espaço vazio generoso — a distância entre os dois elementos é o conteúdo da peça.
Metadeesquerda
REGISTERED AGENT
Um marcador de endereço simples, sozinho, em cinza-azulado. Abaixo, em corpo pequeno: "an address that receives official mail". Elemento pequeno e isolado.
Metadedireita
YOUR OPERATION
Um agrupamento de elementos — pessoas, transações, contratos — em âmbar, visivelmente maior e mais denso que o marcador da esquerda. Abaixo, em corpo pequeno: "where the business actually happens".
Entreas duas
"These are not the same thing."
No espaço vazio central, em corpo pequeno e branco. Logo KwikLedgers no rodapé.
Legenda
Every US entity needs a registered agent. Almost every foreign founder believes it means more than it does. Here is the actual job. A registered agent is a required point of contact inside the state — an address that can legally receive official documents and service of process on the company's behalf. That's it. It exists so the state and the courts always have somewhere to send things. It is a compliance requirement. It is not a business presence. What it does not do: It does not give your company an operating presence in that state. It does not determine where your business is considered to be doing business — that follows your activity, not your mailbox. It does not create substance for any purpose that asks about substance. It does not file anything on your behalf. Most agents forward mail; they don't act on it. Two consequences follow from that, and both catch people. The first is strategic. Choosing a state because the agent is cheap there doesn't change where you actually operate, and it doesn't change what that activity triggers elsewhere. The second is boring and worse. Official notices go to the agent's address. If the agent contract lapses, or the forwarding address is one you stopped checking three years ago, the notices still get delivered — legally, to you — and you never see them. Deadlines included. The agent is a mailbox with legal standing. It's worth knowing which of your assumptions are resting on it. Do you know what your registered agent actually does for your company — and what it doesn't?
Validação fiscal obrigatória. Confirmar antes de publicar: a descrição do papel do registered agent; a afirmação de que o local de operação segue a atividade e não o endereço do agente; e se o time quer ou não entrar no mérito de escolha de estado, que aqui aparece de forma genérica.

The company you stopped using didn't stop filing

29/set (ter) · publicar 9h–10h ET (10h–11h BRT) · PDF vertical 1080×1350 · 8 páginas

Páginas do PDF — texto na peça + direção de arte
01Capa
"You stopped using the company. It never stopped having obligations."
Fundo escuro, duas frases empilhadas, a segunda em âmbar. Sem logo nesta página.
02O cenário
The most common version of this
Texto: "You opened a US entity for a project, a client or a plan. It ran for a while, or never really started. Then priorities changed and you moved on. Nobody ever formally closed it." Arte: uma linha do tempo que começa cheia e vai ficando tracejada até sumir.
03O que
continua
Inactivity is not a status the state recognizes
Texto: "State-level filings and annual fees generally continue for as long as the entity exists, regardless of activity. Federal obligations can continue too — some of them apply to entities with no revenue at all." Arte: engrenagem girando sozinha num fundo vazio.
04O silêncio
The notices arrive. They just don't arrive at you.
Texto: "Official correspondence goes to the registered agent. If that contract lapsed, or the forwarding address is one you no longer check, delivery still counts as made. The obligations accumulate somewhere you are not looking." Arte: caixa de correio cheia, com uma carta caindo fora.
05O primeiro
custo
Losing good standing
Texto: "Good standing is the state's confirmation that the entity is active and current. Without it, banks close or refuse accounts, contracts signed in the company's name get questioned, and the liability protection you opened the entity for gets harder to defend." Arte: selo de "good standing" esmaecendo.
06O segundo
custo
Administrative dissolution
Texto: "Left long enough, the state can dissolve the entity on its own. The name becomes available to whoever wants it. Reinstating is possible in many cases, but it costs more than maintaining ever would have — and it is not always available on the same terms." Arte: nome de empresa riscado, com um cursor de outra pessoa ao lado.
07A saída
Closing is a process, not an absence
Texto: "Properly closing an entity means final filings, formal dissolution with the state, and cancelling the registrations attached to it. It takes work once. Abandoning it takes nothing and costs indefinitely." Arte: duas rotas — uma curta com um ponto final, outra longa e aberta que não termina.
08Pergunta
"Do you have a US entity you no longer use — and do you know what it is still supposed to be filing?"
Fundo claro, pergunta grande, logo KwikLedgers discreto no rodapé.
Legenda
There is a specific kind of problem that only shows up years later, and it starts with a decision nobody remembers making. You opened a US entity. For a project, a client, a plan that made sense at the time. It ran for a while, or it never really started. Priorities changed, you moved on, and nobody ever formally closed it. In your head, the company stopped existing the day you stopped using it. Not to the state. Inactivity isn't a status. State filings and annual fees generally continue for as long as the entity exists, regardless of whether it earned a dollar. Some federal obligations continue too — including ones that apply specifically to entities with no activity. And here is what makes it quiet. Official correspondence goes to your registered agent. If that contract lapsed, or the forwarding address is one you stopped checking three years ago, delivery still counts as made. Legally, you were notified. In practice, nothing reached you. So the first thing you lose is good standing — the state's confirmation that the company is current. Without it, banks close or refuse accounts, contracts signed in the company's name get questioned, and the liability protection you opened the entity for becomes harder to defend. The second thing you can lose is the company. Left long enough, the state may dissolve it administratively, and the name goes back on the shelf. Reinstating is often possible. It just costs more than maintaining ever would have. None of this is dramatic while it's happening. That's the point — there is no moment where something goes wrong loudly enough to get your attention. Closing an entity properly is a process: final filings, formal dissolution with the state, cancelling what's attached to it. It takes work once. Abandoning it takes nothing, and keeps costing. Do you have a US entity you no longer use — and do you know what it's still supposed to be filing?
